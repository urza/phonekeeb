How often does the finger draw a figure eight?

The original keyboard is called 8pen because a good letter pair traces an eight. This measures how often that actually happens, per layout, in English and Czech.

  • urza wins Czech clearly, 16.1% of weighted bigram mass against 13.9% for its own baseline and 8.5% for the original 8pen.
  • The English gain is inside the noise. urza is +0.3 points on subtitles and −0.4 on Norvig, with the same 19 pairs either way.
  • Nothing here is optimized. Every layout sits near the p95 of a random shuffle, and 2000 blind shuffles already found 26.6%.
  • urza types "this" as one unbroken serpentine, three eights in a row, the most common word in either list that flows end to end.

What counts as an eight

A letter is one closed loop. The finger leaves the center into an entry sector, rotates one way across N arm crossings, and comes back from its landing sector. A bigram joins the landing sector of the first letter to the entry sector of the second. Two things describe that join, and an eight needs both.

Shape is through when the two sectors are opposite, turn when they are adjacent, reverse when they are the same. Curl is counter when the two rotations differ, co when they match. Only through plus counter reads as an eight.

All four panels below start with the same first loop, the letter i on the urza layout: out into East, counter-clockwise, back in from North. Only the second loop changes.

THE REAL FINDING

All three layouts beat the average shuffle, so none is accidental. But urza's English 21.0% sits barely above the shuffle p95 of 18.7%, and 2000 blind tries already found 26.6%. No layout here is optimized for flow, and a real search has room to beat all of them.

Data

Letter n-grams come from the OPUS OpenSubtitles v2018 dumps already in the repo, built by tools/build-letter-ngrams.py. Within-word pairs only, because a space puts the finger back in the center. The apostrophe splits a word. Czech diacritics fold to base letters before counting, since the end state is one combined en+cs layout.

No published letter-bigram table exists for Czech, which is why both languages are derived by one method here. English has a cross-check against Norvig's Google Books table: 83 of our top 100 pairs are in his top 100, Spearman 0.61 on the shared set. The 17 that differ are the spoken/literary split, not an error. We gain yo do go ay oo gh ck ey and lose io ns ec pr ct tr nc. Published sources put the top 100 at about 76% of bigram frequency; our English tables measured 76.7%.

What to do next

  1. Keep the a/s swap. It is clearly right for Czech and neutral for English, and Czech is the half that needs help.
  2. Do not tune further by hand. Single swaps move the number by tenths of a point and have side effects in both directions. The measurement is now cheap, so a search is the right tool.
  3. Run a real optimization over the combined en+cs weighted eight share, under the ring constraint that keeps frequent letters cheap. Beating 26.6% should be comfortable.
  4. Do not optimize eights alone. Ring cost and reversal share matter too. A layout that maximizes eights while pushing common letters to ring 4 would be worse to type.
  5. Type the original 8pen layout for a while. It has the fewest eights and the fewest reversals, 55.7% soft corners. The felt experience may not match the metric, and that is worth knowing before optimizing hard against the metric.
